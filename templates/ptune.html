Mathematically, this reduces the number of trainable parameters from \( |\theta| \) (total model parameters) to \( p \times e \), where \( p \times e \ll |\theta| \), significantly lowering computational overhead.

The ease of adaptation of language models in recent years can be largely attributed to the development of new techniques involving insertion of low-rank matrices into the transformer blocks and techniques such as freezing model parameters for pre-trained models and learning task-specific weightings for the pre-layer representations. Prefix Tuning

Prefix tuning freezes the model parameters and back propagates the error through the prefix activations in each of the encoder blocks of the transformers.

Here, prefix vectors \( P_k \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d_k} \) and \( P_v \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times d_v} \) are introduced to the attention mechanism, where \( m \) is the prefix length, and \( d_k \), \( d_v \) are dimensions of keys and values, respectively, optimizing only these while keeping \( \theta \) fixed.

Prompt Tuning

In prompt tuning, we freeze the model parameters and allow \( k \) additional tunable parameters in the downstream tasks. This soft prompt is tuned end-to-end and can condense the signal from a fully labeled dataset. Prompt tuning becomes more competitive with scale, allowing task-specific parameters to be derived from generalist parameters. It also supports prompt ensembling, where multiple tasks can be learned from the same prompt, improving quality and robustness as the model scales.

The prompt \( P_e \) is optimized to maximize \( p(y \mid X, P_e; \theta) \), often using a cross-entropy loss \( \mathcal{L} = -\sum y_i \log p(y_i \mid X, P_e; \theta) \).

On domain shift problems, prompt tuning outperforms traditional methods. This approach also highlights the importance of prompt ensembling for better generalization.

Mathematical Formulation

The task involved here is the probability of classifying if \( p(y \mid X; \theta) \), where \( y \) is the class label, \( X = \{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\} \) is the input sequence of tokens, and \( \theta \) represents the model's parameters.

The input \( X \) is embedded as \( X_e \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times e} \), concatenated with \( P_e \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times e} \) to form \( [P_e; X_e] \in \mathbb{R}^{(p + n) \times e} \), and processed to compute \( p(y \mid [P_e; X_e]; \theta) \).

Initialization and Training

There are many possible ways to initialize the prompt representations. One can train from scratch with random initializations or initialize the prompt representations from words drawn from the model's vocabulary. In practice, initializing with specific embeddings from valid tokens can prime the model to restrict output sequences to legal classes.

For example, \( P_e \) might be initialized with random values \( \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 0.1) \) or embeddings of tokens sampled from the vocabulary \( V \).

The existence of decoding priors influences how the sentinels of the vocabulary are stimulated, especially since pretraining is exclusively on span corruption. For T5 models, this involves converting masked tokens to sentinel tokens, which can limit diversity in responses due to the constrained vocabulary.

This efficiency stems from tuning only \( p \times e \) parameters versus \( |\theta| \), often orders of magnitude larger.

For prompt length and initialization, a variable approach is used, selecting prompts of sizes like 1, 5, 20, or 100 tokens. Initialization is often done by sampling uniformly in the range [-0.5, 0.5], with the model's vocabulary restricted to the 5,000 most common tokens. This restriction ensures that the model only looks for words outside the context when necessary, controlling the diversity of generated responses.

However, challenges remain, such as the occasional generation of empty strings in medium-scale models due to limited vocabulary and the non-deterministic nature of language models. Larger models trained for at least 100,000 steps tend to be more reliable in such cases.

Comparison with Other Approaches

Previous studies have explored similar techniques, such as feeding the entire vocabulary into models or using masking tokens with learnable output layers for classification. However, these methods often lack flexibility, especially for language generation tasks. Other approaches, like inserting adaptor layers between transformer blocks, can be more costly than prompt tuning in the long run.

By operating at the prompt level, prompt tuning reduces the risk of overfitting, as it avoids memorizing specific lexical cues or spurious correlations—a common issue in fine-tuning. Additionally, since prompts work in the continuous embedding space, they can be less intuitive than discrete tokens, but clustering approaches show that similar tokens often group together in this space.

For example, embeddings in \( \mathbb{R}^e \) can be clustered using algorithms like k-means to reveal semantic similarities.
